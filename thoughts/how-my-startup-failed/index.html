How My Startup Failed

Hashem AlyMay 23, 20203 min read

Going into a new tech venture you know there’s a slim chance it’ll succeed, but do it anyway because you believe in something, in an idea and a problem that needs to be solved. Ever since I can remember I’ve always wanted to solve the cleaning issue in Egypt. Those who live here know the inefficiency and lack of professionalism that comes along with trying to clean your home. So identifying the problem was not difficult when I began Tandeefa, a home cleaning app that connects cleaners with households. However, 18 months and many decisions later we are forced to shut our doors. I will try to shed light on some of the issues I faced and what I’ve learnt.

Pick the Right Team

Once you have an idea, the next crucial step would be picking the right team. We all know that trying to do everything yourself will lead you nowhere. If you pick the right team, you’ll come up with the right strategies, do the right marketing, pick the right products to sell and features to build. This part is crucial and any shortcuts here may lead to issues going forward, even if these problems aren’t clear in the beginning.

Once you have the right team, be sure to really think through the equity this team gets. Most startups are strapped on cash and attracting talent as co-founders and early employees with huge salaries is usually out of the question, which means you’ll have to give away equity. It’s also very crucial to really think about how much equity you’re giving a person and in return for what. Something like a quick 50/50 split or an equal three way split usually means you haven’t really thought things through. It may seem all good and dandy at first, but this will only create tension going forward and is a disaster waiting to happen.

Don’t Commit to Creating a Company Too Soon

Entrepreneurship is a lot of trial and error and it will take many iterations to get things right. Not only that, but you may be building something that people don’t really need. You will need to be 100% dedicated with your time and effort to make sure your idea comes to reality but that doesn’t mean committing by creating a company. One of the traps I fell into really early on was creating a company before validating the idea, which was problematic because it boxed us in, not giving room for flexibility, or to pivot in other directions. Not only that, but there will be the constant pressure of the taxation, employee legalities, and so on. If you have that pressure on your shoulders without clearly seeing the momentum and growth that validates the idea, it can take a toll on you and limit your ability to pursue other ideas and opportunities. So don’t commit to starting a company until you’re as sure as you can be that your idea is going to be successful. Be as flexible and nimble as possible early on.

Black Swans

Black Swans are unpredictable events that cannot be foreseen and have severe consequences. In our case, it was the COVID-19 outbreak, and it was the nail in our coffin. We had our regular issues of funding, unit economics, bootstrapping, and product-market fit before COVID-19, but starting March 2020, as the virus began to spread, demand decreased dramatically, people weren’t willing to bring cleaners into their homes. Such events occur every 100 years but, unfortunately, if you don’t already have a profitable business or are heavily funded these types of events will have very negative consequences on your business.

Conclusion

Startups are extremely challenging and at times it can feel unbearable. Do what you can to minimize your chances of failure; your mindset for the first few months, year, or two should be survival mode. Make sure you have the right idea, team, and strategies before committing too much effort or money. One of the most crucial things I learnt is to know when to quit. Your energy can be better spent elsewhere doing incredible things that are waiting for your input and disruption. Choose the right horses and ride them well.
